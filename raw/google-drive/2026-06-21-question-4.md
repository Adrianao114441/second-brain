# Question 4 — Edexcel IGCSE English Language A, Paper 1

## What is Question 4?

Question 4 (12 marks) is the language and structure analysis question in Paper 1 Section A. It is always based on one of the 10 anthology texts from Part 1 of the Pearson Edexcel IGCSE English Anthology (non-fiction).

It tests AO2: understanding and analysing how writers use linguistic and structural devices to achieve their effects.

**Question types:**
- Type A: "How does the writer use language and structure to interest and engage the reader?"
- Type B: "How does the writer use language and structure to show the writer's thoughts and feelings?"
- Type C: "How does the writer use language and structure to describe their experiences?" (less common)

## The 10 Anthology Texts

### 1. A Passage to Africa — George Alagiah

**Question type:** How does the writer use language and structure to interest and engage the reader?

Key points: Vivid emotive imagery of famine; first-person confessional tone; structural focus on a single face; contrast between detachment and the smile; rhetorical questions; short sentences for pacing; chronological structure building to a haunting moment; journalistic precision mixed with literary language.

### 2. The Explorer's Daughter — Kari Herbert

**Question type:** Interest and engage / show thoughts and feelings?

Key points: Sensory atmospheric description of Arctic landscape; ethical conflict as structural engine; dual perspective between objectivity and personal feeling; narwhal elevated to almost mythic status; present-tense immediacy.

### 3. H is for Hawk — Helen Macdonald

Key points: Rich nature writing with technical falconry vocabulary; grief as a subtext beneath the surface action; intimacy created by second-person address at moments; juxtaposition of wild hawk and domesticated self.

### 4. Beyond the Sky and the Earth — Jamie Zeppa

Key points: Fish-out-of-water narrative; contrast between expectation and reality of Bhutan; lyrical description of landscape; personal vulnerability and humour.

### 5. Explorers or Boys Messing About? — Tom Morris

**Question type:** How does the writer convey their opinion of the explorers?

Key points: Headline as sarcasm; anti-climaxes undercut heroism; loaded language; irony; mimics adventure story structure only to undermine it.

### 6. Between a Rock and a Hard Place — Aron Ralston

Key points: Visceral physical description; mounting dread through precise detail; time structure creating countdown pressure; rare moments of dark humour.

### 7. A Game of Polo with a Headless Goat — Emma Levine

Key points: Energetic pace; vivid sensory detail of the race; outsider perspective making familiar seem exotic; humour through understatement.

### 8. Chinese Cinderella — Adeline Yen Mah

Key points: Child narrator creating dramatic irony; contrast between achievement and family rejection; emotionally restrained tone heightening sadness.

### 9. The Danger of a Single Story — Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie

**Question type:** Conveys thoughts and perspectives.

Key points: Personal anecdotes transforming abstract ideas; self-juxtaposition admitting her own prejudice; inclusive "we"; repetition of "single story" as motif; structure from personal memory to global politics; rhetorical questions; metaphor of stories as lenses.

### 10. Young and Dyslexic? You've Got It Made — Benjamin Zephaniah

Key points: Conversational, defiant tone; personal anecdote of struggle and triumph; direct address empowering the reader; short punchy sentences mirroring spoken word style.
